When a new team comes together, start by discussing why the team exists and what it should achieve first. Introductions and task assignments alone leave plenty of room for different expectations.

An inception deck gives that conversation a structure. Jonathan Rasmusson describes it as ten questions for establishing shared direction and expectations at the start of a project. His [original article](https://agilewarrior.wordpress.com/2010/11/06/the-agile-inception-deck/) explains the approach.

The suggestions below are for a new team working on a shared project. The example and meeting agenda are proposals for this article.

## Start with a purpose and a first outcome

Imagine a team of developers, designers, and support staff brought together to improve customer support. This is a fictional example.

Everyone wants fewer support requests, but they may mean different things. Support staff might want to spend less time answering repeated questions. Developers might be thinking about bugs. A designer might want to fix a confusing screen.

All of those sound useful. They would lead to different first tasks, though. Ask which requests the team wants to reduce, who is struggling, and what would change if the work succeeded.

A first outcome might be: people who have trouble signing in can find a way to resolve it themselves. That gives the team something concrete to discuss. Should they change the screen, add guidance, or examine support requests first?

## Use the ten questions to find gaps

These are brief paraphrases of the ten topics in Rasmusson's deck. Look for questions that people answer differently, or cannot answer yet.

| Topic | What to establish |
| --- | --- |
| Why are we doing this? | Purpose |
| How would we explain it briefly? | Audience and value |
| What would go on the product box? | The promise to users |
| What will we leave out? | Scope |
| Who do we need to work with? | Other people and teams |
| How might we build it? | An approach |
| What worries us? | Risks |
| How big is it? | A rough sense of scale |
| What takes priority? | Constraints and room to adjust |
| What will we need? | People, time, and funding |

For the support example, the scope might say that automating the whole support operation is outside the first piece of work. That helps everyone check the boundaries of the discussion.

## Bring background information to the meeting

The facilitator should share the reason for the request, relevant feedback from users, and any fixed deadlines. Include both the people asking for the work and the people doing it. Invite operations or support staff when their input is needed.

Separate decisions already made from questions the team can still discuss. If the budget is fixed, say so before people make plans that depend on changing it.

If the facilitator brings an outline, label it as a starting point. People who still have questions should have room to question it.

## A first 90-minute conversation

Here is one possible agenda. It is a first conversation, with no expectation of completing the whole deck in 90 minutes.

| Time | Activity |
| --- | --- |
| 0 to 10 minutes | Share the background and fixed constraints |
| 10 to 20 minutes | Write individually about the purpose and first outcome |
| 20 to 45 minutes | Read the answers and discuss differences |
| 45 to 65 minutes | Discuss the initial scope and concerns |
| 65 to 80 minutes | Record decisions, open questions, and things to check |
| 80 to 90 minutes | Assign follow-up actions and agree when to meet again |

Writing separately first gives people a chance to form an answer before hearing the first speaker. Ask each person to read theirs. Keep different answers visible for a while before combining them into a shared statement.

If the discussion gets too broad, ask whether the team needs to decide that point now and what information would help. Technical details may need a separate session. Give that follow-up an owner and a date.

## Keep the reasons behind a disagreement

Different answers do not always call for a vote. People may have different information or constraints.

Suppose one person wants to meet a launch date and another wants the full feature set ready before release. Ask why. The date might be tied to an external event; the features might be part of a promise to users. With those reasons on the table, the team can consider a smaller first release.

If the team cannot decide, turn the question into an action, such as asking the sponsor whether the launch date is fixed. Record who will ask and when. When discussing quality, name the conditions that must hold, such as protecting personal data or passing specific tests.

## Discuss everyday working arrangements too

The inception deck mainly addresses the direction of a project. A new team also needs to discuss how it will work each day.

Who sets priorities? Where do people ask for help? How do they request a review? Before calling work complete, who checks what?

Begin with the arrangements the team will need for its first tasks and revisit them after some actual work. A specific agreement is easier to use: post urgent questions in chat, then record the resulting decision in the task.

Rasmusson's [material on starting teams](https://rasmusson.wordpress.com/wp-content/uploads/2008/01/rasmusson-agileinceptiondeckbootcamp.pdf) also discusses roles and team practices.

## End with a next step people can take

Before closing, ask participants to explain the decisions in their own words. The wording can differ as long as they mean the same audience and first outcome. If they still mean different things, leave that point open.

Record the decisions, unanswered questions, and follow-up actions with names and dates. Share the notes with people who could not attend. They should be able to tell what happens next.

For the support team, that first task might be for a developer and a support colleague to read recent sign-in requests together. They can bring what they learn to the next conversation and narrow the scope. The purpose and scope written at the start can change as the team learns more.
